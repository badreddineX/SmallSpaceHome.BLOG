A studio apartment is a single room that has to function as a bedroom, living room, home office, and dining room simultaneously. In Canadian cities where a studio is often the most affordable rental option — particularly in Toronto, Vancouver, and Montreal — getting the layout right makes the difference between a space that works and one that feels like a problem to solve every day.

This guide covers the full setup: how to zone the space, what furniture works at studio scale, storage solutions for a single-room apartment, and how to make it feel like a considered home rather than a temporary solution.

---

## The Core Challenge: One Room, Four Functions

Most studio problems come from trying to do too much in one area rather than defining zones. When the bed is in the middle of the room with the sofa facing it and the desk in the corner, every zone bleeds into every other — it is hard to feel like you are relaxing in the living area when the bed is three feet away.

The goal is functional separation using furniture placement, rugs, and visual cues rather than walls.

---

## Zone 1: The Sleeping Area

The bed is the largest piece of furniture in a studio. How it is positioned determines the entire layout.

### Positioning the bed

**Against the back wall:** Place the bed against the wall farthest from the entrance. This puts the bed at the back, living zone at the front — the natural progression of a home from public (entrance, living area) to private (sleeping zone).

**In a corner:** Pushing the bed into a corner frees two walls of circulation space. The trade-off is that the person sleeping against the wall has to climb over the other person to get out — workable for one person, less practical for two.

**Perpendicular to the wall with a headboard bookshelf:** A bookshelf headboard (IKEA HEMNES headboard, ~$150, or a KALLAX shelf placed behind the bed) creates a visual back to the sleeping zone and adds storage without taking additional floor space.

### Bed type for a studio

**Platform bed with storage** (drawers or hydraulic lift): Essential in a studio where the closet is small or nonexistent. The under-bed space holds clothing, bedding, and seasonal items — removing the need for a separate dresser. See [platform bed with storage guide](/blog/platform-bed-with-storage-canada) for specific Canadian options.

**Daybed with a trundle:** A daybed looks like a sofa during the day and converts to a full bed at night. Works in very small studios (under 32 sq m) or bachelor apartments where even a standard queen bed overwhelms the space. The trundle pulls out to create a second sleeping surface for a guest.

**Murphy bed (wall bed):** A Murphy bed folds up into a wall cabinet during the day, freeing the entire floor for living space. In Canada, Murphy bed systems start at $1,500–$3,000 for a queen size installed. IKEA's KNOPPÄNG wall bed system (~$2,000–$2,500) is the most accessible Canadian option. Best for studios under 30 sq m where any fixed bed takes up proportionally too much floor area.

## Zone 2: The Living Area

The sofa anchors the living zone. In a studio, the sofa does double duty — it is the seating for relaxing, for work video calls, and for watching the TV. Pick it for size first.

### Sofa scale for a studio

**Apartment-depth sofa (80–90 cm deep):** Leaves enough circulation space in front of it in a studio layout. Standard 3-seaters (95–100 cm deep) work only in studios above 38–40 sq m.

**Love seat (2-seater, 130–150 cm wide):** The most practical sofa size for a studio under 35 sq m. Leaves more floor space, is easier to arrange, and rarely feels cramped for one or two occupants.

**Sofa bed:** A sofa that converts to a bed serves as both living and sleeping zone. Most sofa beds are less comfortable than a dedicated bed for regular nightly use, but for a very small studio or for someone who rarely has guests, a quality sofa bed (Article, IKEA FRIHETEN, ~$800–$1,200) is a viable single-piece solution.

### Separating the living zone from the sleeping zone

A bookshelf or open wardrobe placed perpendicular to the wall — or used as a room divider — creates a partial visual separation without blocking light or airflow. IKEA KALLAX (4×2 or 4×4 configuration) placed perpendicular to the wall works well: it has storage on both sides, defines the sleeping zone behind it, and is light enough to move if the layout needs to change.

A rug under the sofa and coffee table anchors the living zone as a distinct area. The rug does not need to be large — enough to cover the seating zone clearly separates it from the sleeping zone at the back of the room.

## Zone 3: The Dining Area

Studios in Canada often have either a galley kitchen with a narrow peninsula, or a separate kitchen alcove with no room for a table. Dining in a studio requires a compact approach.

### Folding or extendable dining table

A folding table (IKEA NORBERG wall-mounted folding table, ~$60) mounts to the wall and folds flat when not in use. When open, it holds two place settings. When closed, it is 10 cm deep — invisible against the wall. Best for studios where eating at a table is occasional rather than daily.

An extendable dining table (IKEA LISABO, ~$200 at compact size) sits small when closed (70 × 70 cm) and extends to 70 × 118 cm for guests. Stores two chairs underneath when not in use.

### Bar stools at a kitchen peninsula

If the studio has a kitchen counter with enough overhang (25+ cm), two bar stools at the counter replace a dining table entirely. This is the most space-efficient dining setup in a studio — no extra floor space, chairs tuck under the counter when not in use.

## Zone 4: The Work Area

Working from home in a studio requires a desk that does not take over the room. A 100–120 cm wide desk with a simple chair is sufficient for most people — the desk does not need to be large if the work is primarily screen-based.

**Position:** Place the desk in a corner or against a wall not visible from the sofa — this prevents work bleeding into the living zone visually. If the desk is in eyeshot of the sofa, the brain never fully switches off from work mode.

**Desk options for a studio:**
- IKEA MICKE (73 × 50 cm, ~$100): Compact, built-in storage, fits most studio corners
- IKEA LINNMON + ADILS legs (~$60–$80 for a 100 × 60 cm top + 4 legs): Customizable depth and width, very affordable
- [Floating wall desk](https://www.amazon.ca/dp/B0D78FBSWK?tag=smallspace06f-20) (Amazon.ca, ~$80–$150 CAD): Mounts to the wall, folds flat when not needed — best for studios where floor space is extremely tight

## Storage Strategy for a Studio

A studio apartment has no separate bedroom closet, hallway storage, or spare room. Every storage zone must do more than in a multi-room apartment.

**The storage hierarchy for a studio:**
1. Under the bed (largest volume — use a platform bed with storage)
2. Wardrobe or closet system (IKEA PAX freestanding)
3. Vertical wall shelves above furniture
4. Ottoman with interior storage (replaces coffee table + adds hidden storage)
5. Bookshelf room divider (storage on both sides)

## Making a Studio Look Intentional

The difference between a studio that looks like a furnished room and one that looks like a home is almost entirely about visual cohesion rather than volume of furniture.

**Three-colour rule:** One dominant colour (wall or sofa), one secondary (rug), one accent (pillows, plants, a throw). Studios with too many competing colours feel chaotic regardless of how well-organized they are.

**Consistent leg height on furniture:** When every piece of furniture has legs at a similar height — 15–20 cm — the floor reads as open and the room appears larger. A mix of legged and legless furniture makes the space feel fragmented.

**One large rug, not multiple small ones:** A single rug under the sofa and coffee table anchors the living zone as one area. Small scattered rugs make a studio look like multiple incomplete rooms.

**Plants at different heights:** A floor plant in one corner and a shelf plant at mid-height add life and draw the eye upward without requiring structural changes.
