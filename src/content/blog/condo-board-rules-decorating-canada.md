You own your condo unit. You pay your mortgage, your maintenance fees, and your property taxes. So you should be able to decorate however you want, right?

Not exactly. Condo ownership in Canada is different from owning a detached house. You own the interior of your unit — generally the space from the back of the drywall inward — but you share walls, hallways, roofs, and mechanical systems with every other owner in the building. A condo board (or strata council in BC) exists to manage those shared elements, and the rules they set can reach into your unit in ways that surprise first-time buyers.

This guide breaks down what Canadian condo boards typically control, what they do not, and where the lines get blurry. Every section references the relevant provincial legislation so you know what is law versus what is just your building's preference.

## The Legal Framework: Three Provinces, Three Systems

Canadian condo law is provincial, not federal. The three most relevant statutes for the majority of Canadian condo owners are:

**Ontario — Condominium Act, 1998 (as amended 2017)**
Ontario's Act distinguishes between the declaration (the condo's constitution), bylaws (governance procedures), and rules (day-to-day regulations). Rules can be created or amended by the board, but owners can requisition a meeting to overturn them. The Condominium Authority Tribunal (CAT) handles disputes about pets, vehicles, nuisances, and compliance.

**Quebec — Civil Code of Quebec (articles 1038-1109)**
Quebec treats condos as "divided co-ownership." The declaration of co-ownership includes an act constituting the co-ownership, bylaws of the immovable, and a description of the fractions. Decisions about common elements require a vote of co-owners. The syndicate (equivalent to a condo board) manages the building but must follow the Civil Code's framework.

**British Columbia — Strata Property Act (SPA)**
BC uses the term "strata" instead of "condo." The SPA gives strata councils authority to create bylaws (not just rules), which are enforceable with fines up to $200 per infraction per week for standard bylaws. Owners can challenge bylaws at the Civil Resolution Tribunal (CRT).

Other provinces — Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick — follow similar structures with their own acts. The principles below apply broadly, but always check your province's specific legislation.

## What You Can Do Without Board Approval

These changes fall within your unit boundaries and do not affect common elements or shared systems. In the vast majority of Canadian condos, no approval is needed:

**Painting interior walls.** The walls inside your unit are yours to paint any colour. The only exception: some declarations restrict colours visible through windows from the exterior. If your building has floor-to-ceiling windows facing a street, check whether a "uniform exterior appearance" clause exists.

**Hanging art, shelves, and mirrors.** Putting nails or screws into your interior walls is a standard part of living in your unit. For tips on doing this without damage — especially useful if you plan to sell — see our [no-damage decor guide for Canadian renters](/blog/renters-no-damage-decor-guide-canada), which works just as well for owners who want reversible options.

**Replacing furniture and soft furnishings.** Couches, beds, curtains, rugs, dining tables — all of these are personal property, not fixtures. No board has jurisdiction over your furniture choices.

**Swapping light fixtures (with caveats).** Replacing a ceiling light or vanity light with a new fixture of the same type and wiring is generally fine. You are not altering the electrical system — you are changing a fixture. However, adding new electrical outlets, moving switches, or installing pot lights involves electrical permits and likely board notification.

**Installing window treatments.** Blinds, curtains, and roller shades are yours to choose. Some buildings require that the backing visible from outside be white or neutral — this is a common and enforceable rule tied to uniform exterior appearance.

**Updating cabinet hardware.** Replacing knobs and pulls on kitchen and bathroom cabinets is cosmetic and requires no approval.

## What Almost Always Needs Board Approval

These changes either affect common elements, shared systems, or have the potential to impact neighbouring units:

### Flooring Changes

This is the single most common source of condo board disputes in Canada. Replacing carpet with hardwood, laminate, or tile changes the sound transmission properties of your floor — which is the ceiling of the unit below you.

Most condo declarations or rules require:
- A minimum Sound Transmission Class (STC) rating, typically STC 50 or higher
- A minimum Impact Insulation Class (IIC) rating, often IIC 50 or higher
- Use of an approved underlay or acoustic membrane
- Written board approval before work begins

Ignoring this requirement is one of the fastest ways to receive a compliance order. The unit below you will hear the difference, they will complain, and the board will act.

### Plumbing Modifications

Moving or adding plumbing fixtures — relocating a kitchen sink, adding a bathroom, or rerouting drain lines — affects the building's shared plumbing stack. A leak from your modification can damage multiple units below. Boards require:
- Detailed plans from a licensed plumber
- Proof of insurance
- A pre-work inspection in many buildings
- Restoration of common elements if stacks are accessed

### Electrical Panel Work

Your unit's electrical panel connects to the building's main distribution. Any work beyond swapping fixtures — adding circuits, upgrading panels, running new wiring — requires both board approval and a municipal electrical permit.

### HVAC Modifications

Many Canadian condos use shared HVAC systems: central boilers, fan-coil units, or make-up air systems. You cannot:
- Replace a fan-coil unit with a different model without approval
- Install a mini-split system if it requires exterior condenser mounting (that is a common element)
- Block or modify in-suite ventilation ducts connected to the building system
- Add a dryer vent through an exterior wall

Even in buildings where each unit has its own furnace, modifications that penetrate the building envelope (exterior walls, roof) require approval because the envelope is a common element.

### Removing or Modifying Walls

Load-bearing or not, interior wall removal in a condo typically requires board approval plus engineering sign-off. Even non-load-bearing walls may contain shared mechanical or electrical runs. The process usually involves:
- An engineer's letter confirming the wall is non-structural
- Board review of plans
- Compliance with noise and construction-hours bylaws during work

## Balcony Rules: The Most Misunderstood Area

Your balcony feels like yours. You walk out onto it from your living room. You put your morning coffee out there. But in Canadian condo law, balconies are almost universally classified as **exclusive-use common elements**. You have exclusive access, but the board owns and controls the space.

This means the board can regulate:

**Furniture type and quantity.** Many boards restrict balcony furniture to purpose-built outdoor pieces. Storing indoor furniture, boxes, bikes, or other items on balconies is commonly prohibited under "storage on common elements" rules.

**BBQs and cooking.** Ontario's Fire Code prohibits propane and charcoal BBQs on balconies of buildings over three storeys. Electric BBQs are allowed unless your condo's rules go further and ban all cooking appliances. BC and Quebec have similar fire code restrictions.

**Satellite dishes and antennas.** Most condo rules prohibit exterior-mounted dishes. In buildings that provide bulk cable or internet, the board may argue that individual dishes are unnecessary and affect exterior appearance.

**Holiday decorations and lights.** Seasonal decorations are a grey area. Some boards allow them during specific date ranges (e.g., December 1 to January 15). Others prohibit anything attached to railings or exterior surfaces year-round.

**Planters and gardens.** Overflowing planters can cause water damage to the balcony membrane and the unit below. Boards commonly restrict planter size, require drip trays, and prohibit hanging planters on the exterior side of railings.

**Drying laundry.** Hanging laundry on balcony railings is prohibited in most Canadian condo corporations. This is both an aesthetic rule and, in some cases, a fire code concern (fabric near an exterior wall or railing can spread fire between floors).

## Noise Bylaws and Construction Hours

Every condo corporation in Canada has noise rules, and they affect decorating projects directly. Common restrictions include:

- **Construction hours:** Typically weekdays 9 AM to 5 PM or 8 AM to 6 PM. Saturday work is allowed in some buildings with reduced hours. Sunday and statutory holiday work is almost always prohibited.
- **Noise transmission:** Beyond construction, permanent changes like hard flooring (covered above) are regulated because of ongoing noise impact.
- **Moving and delivery:** Large furniture deliveries may need to be booked through management to reserve the service elevator. Some buildings charge a refundable deposit for elevator padding.

In buildings with thin concrete slabs — common in 1960s-1980s construction across Toronto, Montreal, and Vancouver — noise rules tend to be stricter because the building itself transmits more sound.

## Common Prohibited Changes (Most Buildings)

These modifications are prohibited in the majority of Canadian condo corporations, regardless of province:

1. **Installing a washer/dryer where none existed.** Requires plumbing, electrical, and venting — all touching common systems.
2. **Enclosing a balcony.** Converting a balcony to interior space changes the building envelope and requires engineering approval, municipal permits, and board consent. Most boards deny these requests.
3. **Changing exterior doors or windows.** These are common elements. Even if your sliding door is drafty and 30 years old, the board controls replacement timing and contractor selection.
4. **Adding a bidet or second toilet.** Any new plumbing fixture connects to the shared stack and requires approval.
5. **Mounting anything to the exterior of the building.** This includes window-mounted AC units (in buildings without central air), flag holders, exterior cameras, and awnings.

## How to Get Board Approval When You Need It

The process varies by building, but a successful application typically includes:

1. **Written request** describing the work in detail, with drawings or photos of proposed changes.
2. **Contractor credentials** — proof of insurance, WSIB coverage (in Ontario), and applicable trade licences.
3. **Engineering letter** if structural work is involved.
4. **Timeline and construction schedule** confirming compliance with noise bylaws.
5. **Agreement to restore** common elements to their original condition if accessed during work.
6. **Deposit** — some boards require a refundable damage deposit for corridor and elevator protection during renovation.

Approval timelines range from two weeks to two months depending on the board's meeting schedule. Budget this time into your project planning.

## What To Do If You Disagree With a Rule

Not every condo rule is reasonable, and not every rule is enforceable. Your options depend on your province:

**Ontario:** The Condominium Authority Tribunal (CAT) handles disputes about compliance with declarations, bylaws, and rules. Filing is done online and costs start at $25. The CAT can order compliance, but it can also find that a rule is unreasonable.

**Quebec:** Disputes go to the Superior Court of Quebec. Co-owners can argue that a bylaw is contrary to the destination of the immovable (the building's intended use as residential) or is abusive. Quebec courts have struck down rules that unreasonably restrict an owner's enjoyment.

**BC:** The Civil Resolution Tribunal (CRT) handles strata disputes up to $5,000 in value. For larger disputes, the BC Supreme Court has jurisdiction. The CRT can order a strata to amend or repeal a bylaw that is "significantly unfair."

Before going to a tribunal, start with a written request to the board explaining your position. Many disputes are resolved at this stage because boards prefer to avoid the cost and time of formal proceedings.

## Tips for Decorating a Canadian Condo Without Board Issues

1. **Read your declaration and bylaws first** — not a summary, not what your real estate agent told you, but the actual documents. They are legally binding and override anything else.
2. **Ask in writing** before starting any project you are unsure about. An email to property management creates a paper trail.
3. **Photograph your unit before and after** any modification. This protects you if a future board or buyer disputes the condition.
4. **Keep receipts for materials and contractor invoices.** If a board challenges your work, proof of professional installation and code-compliant materials strengthens your position.
5. **Talk to your neighbours** before noisy projects. A heads-up goes further than a formal notice in maintaining good relations.
6. **Attend AGMs.** Annual general meetings are where rules get created and amended. If you want influence over what owners can and cannot do, this is where it happens.
